# Five archetype concepts — selection checkpoint

Historical concept packet: the selection language below describes the moment
this packet was prepared. Gates, Delivery and Feeder Choice were subsequently
selected and played; Connections emerged through owner feedback. See the
[current workbench](archetype-workbench.md) for dispositions and remaining work.

Prepared 2026-10-01. These are proposed mechanics and hypothetical decision
scenes, not playable levels or validated findings. The owner has allowed new
mechanics; none of these particular mechanics has been selected yet.

The [working plan](../../plans/2026-10-01-level-archetypes-approach.md) defines
autonomous development and the three owner checkpoints.

## Recommendation

Start with **Gates, Delivery, and Feeder Choice**. They put the emphasis on
connections, clearing space, and directing incoming material respectively.
Keep **Heat** and **Turn the Board** as alternatives. Heat may feel punitive;
changing gravity carries the largest readability risk.

## What the earlier plays change

The owner's baseline is build-and-harvest, documented by captured play in
[Cash or Compound](../../../prototypes/cash-or-compound/README.md). Making
several useful tiles and joining them later is familiar play, not a new family.

The [playtest decision ledger](../../../prototypes/PLAYTEST-DECISION-LEDGER.md)
records four constraints relevant here:

- PDL-005: an unusual opening lost its identity after refill. Each proposed
  mechanic therefore persists as part of the board or its rules.
- PDL-006 and PDL-009: bombs and bonus contracts could be satisfied during
  ordinary play. A displayed objective is insufficient without a changed choice.
- PDL-004: a storage pocket felt like wasted space. Restrictions need a visible
  return that the player can control.
- PDL-002: the fixed-gate board produced a distinct experience for the owner
  on one captured board. It supports exploring connectivity, not assuming the
  new Gates mechanic will work.

## A — Gates

**Rule:** Two fixed passages share a switch. Ending a chain on its marked
switch cell opens one passage and closes the other. The switch acts before
gravity, and the selected passage stays open until switched again.

**Decision scene:** Three small regions hold partly prepared chains. The open
lower passage connects two groups that could be harvested soon. Switching
would close that route and open an upper passage to a useful smaller tile in
the third region. The player can finish the lower harvest first, or give up
that immediate connection to prepare a different chain.

**Why both choices appeal:** One preserves a ready opportunity; the other
improves access to material needed for a different opportunity. Closing a
passage must have a visible cost. Simply opening extra space would make the
switch too easy to use automatically.

**Difference from the baseline:** Building compatible values is no longer
sufficient; the player must choose which can reach one another. This is an
extension of the positive fixed-gate precedent, with player control replacing
automatic thawing. That extension remains unplayed.

**What the player sees:** Two clearly linked passages and a preview of which
will open and close. The board's connection state persists through refills.

**Reject if:** The switch is an obvious chore, one position is always best, or
ordinary harvesting toggles it incidentally. Vary passage locations and which
regions share them only after the basic choice works.

## B — Delivery

**Rule:** A cargo piece cannot join number chains. It falls with gravity and
leaves the board when it reaches its marked bottom exit. The representative
level's goal is to deliver the cargo within the move allowance.

**Decision scene:** Cargo sits above a shaft of numbered tiles. A long legal
chain could finish inside that shaft, replacing the cleared tiles with a
large survivor beneath the cargo. Another route finishes outside the shaft,
letting the cargo fall farther while making a less useful scoring survivor.

**Why both choices appeal:** Building the larger tile can help remove a later
obstruction, but it may delay the cargo now. Clearing underneath immediately
makes delivery progress while spending tiles that could have helped elsewhere.
The authored position must make this future benefit credible, not invent it
in the instructions.

**Difference from the baseline:** A valuable retained tile can be a physical
obstruction. The player sometimes wants an empty route more than a banked tile.
The objective changes what a favorable endpoint means.

**What the player sees:** Cargo, its exit, and a landing preview including the
merged survivor. Cargo remains visible across refills; movement shows progress
directly. Begin with one cargo piece and no additional score quota.

**Reject if:** Cargo drops out during ordinary long chains, or the only useful
action is repeatedly clearing directly underneath it. Vary supporting geometry
and competing cargo routes after the first endpoint choice proves interesting.

## C — Feeder Choice

**Rule:** Two marked columns receive two visible refill packets on the next
move. Before drawing the chain, the player assigns the packets to the columns.
The assignment swaps the packets; it does not create extra material. Other
columns refill normally. Packet preview and consumption must remain visible.

**Decision scene:** The left column contains the beginning of a low-value
bridge. The right has a larger tile waiting for compatible neighbors. One
incoming packet offers mostly small tiles; the other offers larger ones.
Routing each toward a suitable neighbor helps both, but the proposed chain
opens different numbers of spaces in the two columns. The player must choose
the route and the clearing move together.

**Why both choices appeal:** Feeding the almost-ready area may give a quick
harvest. Feeding the weaker area may complete the bridge needed for a later
cross-board chain. There must be a real competition for useful incoming tiles;
the favorable allocation cannot always be obvious.

**Difference from the baseline:** The player can deliberately influence the
material a clearing move will receive. The decision starts before refill,
instead of relying on a lucky refill to rescue a plan.

**What the player sees:** Both packets and their destination columns before
committing. Unused packet entries remain visible for the next refill. The
feeder rule recurs throughout the level, so the opening does not own its identity.

**Reject if:** Matching each packet to its column is automatic, every turn
requires tedious rearrangement, or preview detail overwhelms the board.
The packet length and partial-consumption rules are deferred prototype details;
they must preserve the visible allocation tradeoff. Vary the competing needs,
not just the amount of preview information.

## D — Heat

**Rule:** Large tiles next to one another accumulate visible heat. Separating
or combining them removes that pressure; leaving the group together too long
temporarily locks the overheated tiles. Values and warning timing are shown.

**Decision scene:** Three prepared tiles are clustered for a future harvest,
but a needed bridge is missing. Another setup move could complete it. Taking
that move also spends the cluster's last safe turn. The player can cash out
part of the group, change its arrangement, or commit to the bridge now.

**Why both choices appeal:** A longer preparation can earn a better harvest;
an earlier partial harvest preserves usable space and avoids the lock.

**Difference from the baseline:** Holding valuable tiles has a visible spatial
cost. The player must manage the arrangement and timing of the stored value.
This directly changes familiar play, but may feel like a punishment for it.

**What the player sees:** Heat belongs to the tiles and carries across refills.
Use a reversible lock rather than surprise destruction in the first version.

**Reject if:** The player mostly obeys an alarm, always harvests at the warning,
or cannot reasonably predict how gravity changes heat exposure. Keep this as
a reserve because the risk of busywork is substantial.

## E — Turn the Board

**Rule:** The player can spend a move to rotate gravity a quarter-turn.
A preview shows where existing tiles will settle before committing.
Normal chaining resumes with the chosen gravity direction.

**Decision scene:** Two compatible built groups are separated vertically by
small tiles. Turning gravity would line them up along a side, but would also
break a prepared route elsewhere. Working within the current orientation
preserves that route but may require more setup.

**Why both choices appeal:** A turn can reorganize several future connections
at once, while using a move and disturbing other plans. A normal chain makes
score progress and changes less of the board.

**Difference from the baseline:** The player chooses the board's settling
direction as well as the endpoint of a merge. A large stored tile becomes a
piece to reposition deliberately.

**What the player sees:** A direction arrow and a complete settling preview.
Orientation persists until changed. Early versions avoid complicated internal
walls so the player can predict the effect.

**Reject if:** Rotation is always beneficial, serves only as an emergency
reshuffle, or makes the board harder to read than to plan. This could be a
strong alternative to Gates, but changes the familiar feel more substantially.

## Comparison and first play order

Gates and Turn the Board both change access between tiles; build one first so
the initial collection does not overrepresent rearrangement. Feeder Choice
changes incoming material, while Delivery changes the value of cleared space.
Heat adds pressure to familiar preparation and deserves its own taste decision.

Recommended first play order: Gates, Delivery, then Feeder Choice. Gates has
the closest prior positive precedent. Delivery makes progress physically
visible. Feeder Choice asks the player to learn a larger information display
and should come after the simpler introductions.

Concept selection does not declare these mechanics successful. Before the
playtest checkpoint, each selected idea needs an exact board position where
its competing choices survive ordinary build-and-harvest play. The diagrams
and legal routes for those positions belong to the prototype phase, not a
claim made from the hypothetical scenes above.
